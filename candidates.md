# Candidate pitches — Ridiculous at First Glance, Real by the Second (u-po-pu-ridiculous-at-first-glance-real-by-the-second-9a97d12a)

All candidates are original lines written for this entry. Scored 1–5 against
the house judge's five criteria, in the judge's own order of importance
(RULES.txt judge brief):

1. Pitches a bizarre startup idea that sounds ridiculous at first glance yet makes sense as a real business.
2. Genuinely bizarre: something the reader has not heard before, not a familiar business with a strange adjective bolted on.
3. Business logic holds: the sentence makes clear why someone would pay, so the absurd premise turns out to be a real market.
4. Lands as a pitch: one sentence, within 200 chars, with a turn from laughing to nodding.
5. Writing is concrete and vivid, naming the thing being sold and who buys it rather than trading in abstractions.

## C1 (FINAL, v3)
> We rent cars that are already dented, so nervous drivers pay extra for the one rental nobody can blame them for scratching.

- Length: 123 chars, one line.
- 1) 5 — a car-rental pitch, absurd on its face, that resolves into a real business.
- 2) 5 — renting damaged goods on purpose inverts the whole rental model; nobody has heard this pitch before.
- 3) 5 — the payer (nervous drivers) and the money (pay extra to dodge damage fees) are both explicit; rental damage charges are a real, resented cost.
- 4) 5 — the turn is the sentence's spine: "already dented" (laugh) → "nobody can blame them" (nod).
- 5) 5 — concrete nouns throughout: cars, dented, drivers, rental, scratching; no jargon, no filler.
- Total: 25/25.

> For five dollars, a stranger tells you your startup idea is terrible, because your friends are too polite to say it.

- Length: 116 chars, one line.
- 1) 4 — selling brutal honesty is a real service shape (paid feedback). 2) 4 — fresh angle, but "pay to be insulted" is a known joke format. 3) 3 — founders do pay for honest feedback, yet $5 from a stranger reads as a gag, not a market. 4) 4 — the turn is strong ("too polite to say it"). 5) 4 — five dollars, stranger, startup idea.
- Total: 19/25.

## Decision

C1 wins (25/25): the only candidate at full marks on every criterion. It
inverts an entire industry (rent the damaged car on purpose), names the payer
and the exact money logic (nervous drivers pay extra to escape damage fees),
and its laugh-to-nod turn is built into the premise rather than bolted on.
C2 is the runner-up.

Rejected angles, per the Ling search and the judge brief: "X for Y"
(Uber/Tinder/Netflix for X); pet novelties; prank mail (glitter, potatoes);
selling air / star names / moon deeds; insurance gags; "rent a friend /
family / professional mourner / cuddler" bundles; AI/crypto wrapper jokes;
subscription trivia, vending machines for X, rage rooms; "the real product
is your data" reversals; VC jargon ("capture X% of Y", "disrupt"); and any
familiar business with a strange adjective bolted on.

## Revision history (final line edited at least twice)

### v1 (159 chars)
> We rent out cars that are already dented, so you will never pay for the first scratch — nervous drivers pay extra for a car nobody can blame them for damaging.

- Problem: two clauses say the same thing ("never pay for the first
  scratch" and "nobody can blame them for damaging"), and the em dash
  splits the sentence's momentum. 159 chars carries ~30 chars of dead weight.

### v2 (127 chars)
> We rent out cars that are already dented, so nervous drivers pay extra for the one rental they cannot be blamed for scratching.

- Fix: cut the duplicated damage-fee clause and the em dash; the whole
  payoff now lives in one clause ("the one rental they cannot be blamed
  for scratching"). Problem left: "rent out" is two words where "rent"
  does the job, and "cannot be blamed" is flabbier than "nobody can blame
  them".

### v3 (FINAL, 123 chars)
> We rent cars that are already dented, so nervous drivers pay extra for the one rental nobody can blame them for scratching.

- Fix: "rent out" → "rent"; "they cannot be blamed for scratching" →
  "nobody can blame them for scratching" — shorter, more vivid, and the
  blame now has a subject (nobody) instead of a passive construction.
  Every word earns its place; 123 chars, one sentence, one turn.
